Executive Guide: Portfolio Health Index

Every other index in this framework answers why a particular score should exist. PHI has to answer something harder. Can a business ever be represented by a single number without lying? This guide is an argument that it can, if the number is built the right way.

PHIIs the business healthy?
01

The number executives always ask for

Every board meeting eventually ends with the same request. Give me one number.

It is the most common request in executive reporting, and the most dangerous. Executives ask for one number because their scarcest resource is not data, it is attention, and a single figure is the only thing that survives the gap between a two-hundred-page report and a thirty-minute meeting. The request is not laziness. It is a rational response to complexity, and it is correct.

A leader responsible for a hundred properties cannot hold a hundred occupancy trends in mind, and pretending otherwise produces worse decisions, not better ones. The demand for a single portfolio number is real, persistent, and reasonable. The only question is whether it can be met without lying.

02

Why one number is dangerous

A single number earns its power by throwing information away. That is its purpose and its hazard. The moment a portfolio is reduced to one figure, the figure stops distinguishing between situations that could not be more different. A portfolio that is uniformly mediocre and a portfolio that is excellent in marketing and failing in operations can report the identical composite, and an executive reading only that composite would treat them the same.

Worse, a careless single number destroys accountability. When marketing, operations, and vendor performance are melted into one pooled figure, a decline cannot be traced to a discipline, and a number that no one owns is a number no one fixes. The danger is not that the score is wrong. It is that the score is confidently, untraceably vague.

03

Why most dashboards fail here

The standard dashboard response to this tension is to show everything: forty tiles, a dozen trends, a wall of green and red. It fails for the same reason a single blended average fails, from the opposite direction. A wall of metrics does not answer the executive's question; it hands the question back to them. The leader still has to synthesize, and synthesis under time pressure is exactly the work they needed help with.

So organizations oscillate between two failures. One number that hides too much, or forty numbers that decide nothing. Both leave the executive without the one thing they came for: a place to start. PHI exists in the narrow space between those failures, and it only works if it refuses both.

04

How PHI compresses without lying

The resolution is a distinction most dashboards miss: there is a difference between averaging metrics and synthesizing judgments. Averaging metrics is what produces the untraceable blur. It pools occupancy, spend, click rates, and lease counts into a single soup where no ingredient is recoverable.

PHI does not do that. It never touches a raw metric. It synthesizes three scores that have already been formed, each inside its own discipline, each already accountable. GPI has decided whether marketing is winning its markets. OPI has decided whether operations can sustain occupancy. VPI has decided whether spend is efficient. PHI combines those three conclusions, and only those three. The compression is real, but nothing is lost that cannot be recovered, because every input to PHI is itself a complete, owned answer one level down.

05

Why weighting beats averaging

If PHI combines three scores, the obvious question is how. A simple average would treat marketing, operations, and vendor performance as equally predictive of portfolio health, and they are not. For a leasing business, geographic marketing performance tends to move first; operations is where those wins are kept or lost; vendor performance is a lever on both more than a direct measure of either.

PHI encodes that judgment as weight, roughly forty-five percent GPI, thirty percent OPI, twenty-five percent VPI. The weighting is not a claim that operations matters less than marketing. It is a claim about sequence, about which discipline signals a change in health earliest. Weighted synthesis lets PHI stay a single number while still carrying an argument about how this particular kind of business actually works. An average carries no argument at all.

06

When to trust PHI, and when to leave it

A framework is only honest if it tells you when to stop using it. Trust PHI when the question is where: where in the portfolio to spend the next hour of attention, which region is drifting, whether the business as a whole is holding. For triage and prioritization, one well-built number is not a compromise; it is the correct instrument.

Leave PHI the instant the question becomes why. PHI can tell an executive that a market is deteriorating; it can never tell them that the cause is a vendor buying empty clicks, or a wave of expirations with no pipeline behind them. Those answers live in GPI, OPI, and VPI, and the discipline of the framework is knowing that a falling PHI is an instruction to drill down, not a conclusion to report. PHI points. The three guides beside this one explain.

The centerpiece argument

A business can be represented by a single number, but only if that number is a synthesis of accountable judgments, never an average of raw metrics.

That distinction is the entire reason PHI can be trusted, and the entire reason it must always be read with the three indexes beneath it.

PHI is therefore neither a dashboard nor a KPI. It is an agreement about how an organization chooses to understand itself: a way to reduce complexity without surrendering accountability. Every score in NorthStar, every pillar, every measure, ultimately exists in service of that single idea. Build the number honestly, and one figure can carry the truth of an entire portfolio.

07

Decisions this score enables

The value of PHI is not the health reading. It is the decisions the reading makes possible, once an executive trusts where the number points.

Executive prioritization

Decide which parts of the portfolio get leadership time this week, by health rather than by whoever escalated loudest.

Portfolio comparison

Compare properties, markets, and regions on one consistent health scale, making outliers in either direction immediately visible.

Board reporting

Report portfolio health with a single defensible number that decomposes cleanly into three accountable disciplines when the board asks why.

Capital planning

Direct investment toward the parts of the portfolio where health is at risk or where strength can be scaled, with a clear line back to the discipline driving it.

Questions PHI answers
  • Is the portfolio healthy overall?
  • Which parts of the portfolio need attention first?
  • How do properties, markets, and regions compare on health?
  • Where should capital and leadership attention go next?
