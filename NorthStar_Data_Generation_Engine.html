Synthetic Data Simulation Engine

When this project began, I expected generating the data to be one of the easier parts. It quickly became the hardest. The challenge wasn't producing random records. It was recreating how a real multifamily portfolio behaves over time, where every property has its own personality, every market its own trends, and every metric has to make sense both on its own and in relation to everything around it.

01  Why Build a Simulation Engine?

I could have generated random data in a weekend and built the same Power BI visuals on top of it. That wasn't the goal. I wanted a reporting platform that behaved like something I would actually have used in production, and if the data underneath it wasn't believable, none of the insights would be either. The simulation engine became the foundation that let every dashboard, KPI, and executive index be tested against realistic business conditions. For me, that's the most important part of the project. Not that I used Python, but that I understood why realistic data matters in the first place.

02  Design Philosophy

Building the dashboard was only half of the project. The other half was creating data realistic enough that the dashboard could actually be trusted. That meant treating the generator less like a random data script and more like a business simulation. Every lease, move-in, renewal, marketing campaign, and occupancy change had to behave the way a real apartment portfolio would over time. The business rules aren't decoration on top of the data. They are what makes it behave: leads respond to spend and season, leases are capped by available capacity, occupancy moves only through move-ins and move-outs, and every executive number resolves from the layers beneath it.

The goal was never to generate data. It was to build something that behaved like a real business, at every property, on every day.

03  Simulation Architecture

The engine is a sequence of simulation stages, not an infrastructure diagram. Configuration and business rules set the conditions; property, market, and seasonal layers shape demand; demand drives prospects, prospects drive leasing, leasing drives operations; and cross-domain reconciliation confirms the domains agree before facts are published as canonical truth.

The business simulation pipeline, from configuration to published canonical truth.

04  Marketing Taxonomy & Attribution

Before any marketing number can be trusted, the simulation needs a consistent structure for what a marketing touch is and where it belongs. That structure is the backbone of the whole platform. Every vendor resolves to a channel, every channel produces funnel stages, funnel activity accumulates into prospect journeys, and journeys are what attribution assigns credit across. This is the taxonomy that lets a lead be traced back to the vendor that produced it, and lets the same lease be counted correctly whether marketing, leasing, or operations is asking.

The implemented marketing taxonomy: vendor to channel to funnel to journey to attribution, into the real fact tables.

Production mapping

The simulation models this taxonomy directly through vendor, channel, and funnel-stage identifiers rather than raw web parameters. In a live deployment, the same structure is what marketing teams express through UTM tagging on campaigns. The table below shows how the implemented taxonomy maps to the standard UTM parameters a production system would carry.

Production UTM ParameterNorthStar Taxonomy Equivalent
utm_sourceVendor (Zillow, Google Ads, Meta, and the other nine)

The value of the taxonomy is that it follows one naming standard. Every value comes from a controlled vocabulary, so a tag is never free text and never ambiguous. A person can read a UTM string and know exactly what it means, and a pipeline can parse it into vendor, channel, property, and funnel stage without guessing. The convention is deliberately simple, which is what makes it easy to apply consistently across every campaign.

05  Engine Dependencies

The causal chain runs from seasonality to occupancy. Each stage is produced from the one above it, so a change upstream reaches everything downstream. The chain is not linear at the end: occupancy feeds back through available units and lease capacity to cap how many leases can be signed, which is what keeps occupancy from exceeding a property’s unit count.

The causal chain, with the occupancy-to-capacity feedback loop that bounds leasing.

07  Rule Hierarchy

The engine does not have rules. It has layers of rules, each scoping the one below it. Platform rules set the world; market and property rules localize it; resident and daily rules produce the individual events. A rule at a higher layer constrains everything beneath it.

08  Randomness vs Determinism

The engine uses randomness, but it is not random. Every run is seeded, so the same configuration produces the same portfolio every time. Randomness supplies the natural variation a real business has, the day-to-day noise in leads and conversions, while the seed makes that variation reproducible. This is what lets the platform have a single canonical truth: the generated data is the answer key precisely because it can be regenerated identically and validated against itself. A pipeline reconstruction can then be measured against a fixed target rather than a moving one.

Randomness creates realism. Determinism creates truth. The engine needs both.

10  Why Synthetic Data Is Difficult

This ended up being the hardest part of the entire project, and not because of the amount of code. It was difficult because everything depends on everything else. If I increased lead volume, leasing activity changed. If leasing activity changed, occupancy changed. If occupancy changed, available inventory changed, which affected how many future leases could realistically occur. Even something as small as adjusting a seasonal multiplier could create unrealistic behavior months later. Getting the engine to a point where the data looked believable at the property, market, region, and portfolio levels took months of refinement. The goal was never to make the numbers look good. It was to make them behave the way a real business behaves.

13  Lessons Learned

When people look at NorthStar, they usually notice the dashboards first. What they don't see is that most of the time went into building the data underneath them. Every time one part of the simulation became more realistic, something else usually broke. Fixing one business rule often meant revisiting five others. Over time the project became less about generating data and more about understanding the relationships that make a multifamily business behave realistically. That process changed how I approach data engineering, analytics, and business systems.

14  What This Engine Represents

The engine is not a data schema. It is a working model of how a multifamily business behaves over time. It holds the relationships between marketing, leasing, and operations the way a real portfolio holds them, and it produces a single, reproducible truth that everything else in the platform is measured against. Building it became the most valuable part of the project, because it required understanding not just how to move data, but how a business actually works underneath the numbers.

Anyone can generate rows. The work was building a system where every rule holds at once, across every property and every day, and where the result can be trusted.
