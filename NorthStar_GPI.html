Executive Guide: Geographic Performance Index

A management guide to the Geographic Performance Index. It argues why leasing performance should be judged by geography and against peers, and shows the decisions that judgment makes possible.

GPIAre our markets performing as expected?
01

The executive question

Every leasing organization already knows which markets are busy. Far fewer can say which markets are performing well.

Those are different questions. Busy is a fact about demand. Performing is a judgment about how well an organization is converting its conditions into results. GPI exists to answer the second question, the one budget decisions actually depend on.

02

Why this index exists

Raw performance lies. A market can lease eighty units a month in a high-demand metro where demand alone would carry it, while another leases twenty in a soft, oversupplied submarket where twenty is a genuine achievement. Ranked by volume, the first market wins every time. Judged by how well each is doing relative to what its conditions should produce, the second may be the stronger operation.

Absolute numbers reward size and demand, not skill. A company that allocates budget by volume pours money into markets that are already winning on their own, and starves the markets where good marketing is actually moving the needle. The business problem GPI solves is misallocation caused by mistaking demand for performance.

03

Design philosophy

The alternative to GPI is the scorecard most organizations already use: rank markets by leases, or by cost per lease, and reward the top of the list. That approach fails because it compares markets that are not comparable. A cost per lease that is excellent in one metro is poor in another, so a single leaderboard mixes skill with circumstance and calls the result performance.

GPI takes a different position. Performance is only meaningful relative to conditions, so each market is graded against its peers rather than a fixed target. And because volume bought at any cost is not success, efficiency is weighed alongside outcome, so a market cannot spend its way to a high score. The design choice, in one line: judge each geography against comparable geographies, on results and on the cost of achieving them.

04

The framework

GPI reads three things about a market and balances them. Each is a management idea, not a formula.

Reality

How the market is actually performing on the ground: the leasing outcomes it is producing, taken at face value. This is the anchor that keeps the score honest about results.

Influence

How much of that outcome marketing can credibly claim. A strong result in a market that would have leased itself earns less than the same result in a market that needed the push. This is where GPI separates demand from marketing's contribution.

Efficiency

What the outcome cost to produce, relative to peers. Two markets with the same result are not equal if one spent twice as much. Efficiency is what stops volume from buying a good score.

05

Executive interpretation

A score is only useful if everyone reads it the same way. Here is how to read this one.

A high scoremeans the market is producing strong leasing outcomes that marketing can credibly claim credit for, at a cost that compares well to its peers. It is a candidate for more investment, not just applause.
A low scoremeans the market is lagging comparable markets once demand and spend are accounted for. It is a prompt to investigate, though the cause may sit outside marketing.
A moving scorematters more than the level. A market sliding down its peer ranking over several periods is an early signal that something is changing before it shows up in raw volume.
06

Decisions this score enables

The point of the score is not the number. It is the decision the number makes possible.

Because GPI makes markets comparable on performance rather than size, it turns a pile of leasing reports into a small set of executive moves.

Market prioritization

Decide which markets get leadership attention this quarter, based on where performance is slipping relative to peers rather than where volume happens to be lowest.

Marketing allocation

Move the next marketing dollar toward markets where spend is demonstrably producing leases, and away from markets where volume is really just demand.

Regional comparison

Compare regions on a like-for-like basis, so a soft region's best operator and a strong region's laggard are both visible for what they are.

Performance benchmarking

Set expectations for a market from its peer set rather than from last year's number, giving operators a fair and defensible bar.

07

Common misinterpretations

A framework earns trust by naming its own limits. These are the readings to avoid.

  • A high GPI does not necessarily mean marketing is perfect. It can mean the peer set is weak, and the market is simply the least-bad in a soft region.
  • A low GPI in a strong region is not automatically a failure. A market can outperform the portfolio and still rank low among exceptional peers.
  • GPI is a marketing lens. A low score driven by an operational problem, a staffing gap or a pricing error, should be read in OPI, not fought with more marketing.
08

Relationship to PHI

GPI is the largest single input to the Portfolio Health Index, contributing roughly forty-five percent of it. That weight is a deliberate statement: for a leasing business, geographic marketing performance is the earliest reliable signal of where portfolio health is heading.

But GPI should never be read as the whole story. It measures one discipline and hands one clean number to PHI. A market can post a strong GPI while its operations quietly deteriorate; that is precisely why PHI also carries OPI and VPI. GPI tells an executive whether marketing is winning the market. It does not tell them whether the property can keep the residents it wins.

Questions GPI answers
  • Which markets are outperforming expectations?
  • Where is marketing most efficient?
  • Which markets deserve additional investment?
  • Where is performance lagging peers?
